The leg zone

Burma, the Shan States and Lanna tattooed their men from the waist down, in patterns dense enough that every source reaches for a garment to describe them. The zone's edge runs through the middle of modern Thailand.

Four sources, side by side

Put the sources side by side.

Burma, 1882. Shway Yoe: every up-country man is tattooed from the navel to below the kneecap. Not a single taw-tha, the sturdy man of the small towns and villages, is without it.

The Shan States, 1910. Milne: a Shan boy is a child until he is tattooed; the rule is both legs, waist to knee, and often on to the ankles. Shway Yoe adds that the Shans go further than the Burmese — up the back, to the nape, and among a chief's retainers, everywhere but palms and soles.

Lanna. The Lanna volume: men tattooed until it looked as if they wore black trousers, waist to ankle, torso, arms, fingers — and "this fashion was widespread among the Burmese and the Tai Yai, who tattoo yantras no less than the Lanna."

Siam. Milne, in the same paragraph where she says the Shans learned it from the Burmese: "the Siamese, who are so closely related to them, tattoo themselves very slightly, or not at all."

Inference — that last line is usually read as a fact about Thailand. It is a fact about central Thailand. Milne was comparing the Shan to Bangkok, and Lanna — which was a tributary kingdom, not a province, until 1899 — is on the other side of her comparison. The leg tattoo's territory is the Tai-Buddhist upland: Burma, the Shan States, the Tai Khuen and Tai Lue towns, and Lanna. The edge of the zone runs through modern Thailand, roughly where Lanna ended, and the colonial record could not see it because it was reading the map by kingdoms.

This is what the map on this page is drawing, and it is the one map on the site that no source draws for it.

Counted in the wichaa.net manuscript corpus, a body of northern and central Thai manuscript material transcribed and translated page by page. Thai is written without spaces, so short words match inside longer ones and every count is an upper bound. The tradition that covered the legs of every man in Lanna appears on one page. That is a fact about what manuscripts are for — recipes, katha, yantra, horoscopes — not evidence that the custom was rare. It is also not on this disk in any other form.
